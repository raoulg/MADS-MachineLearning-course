Kernels use the geometric structure of data

In space and in time, positions next to each other belong together: that is how the data hangs together.

neighbours belong together

In a series, the samples next to each other form the beat. In an image, the pixels next to each other form an edge.

dense layer, 400 × 400 grid

160 000 × 160 000
= 25.6 billion weights, one layer, and the neighbours lost.

kernel, 3 × 3

Data has geometry: in a series the samples next to each other belong together, in an image the pixels next to each other. Flatten a 400 by 400 grid and a dense layer of the same size needs 160 thousand times 160 thousand weights, 25.6 billion, and on top of that it has thrown away which cells were neighbours. A kernel that looks at three by three neighbours needs nine weights and reuses them at every position. That reuse is the whole trick of the next part.

■ 01 — how do you get fewer positions?

goal 2.3

notebook 2.1

Fewer positions on an axis: learned, by a fixed rule, or none at all

The tensor is (B, S, D). Each row below shows one slice: one example, one channel, the positions along S.

Downsampling is nothing more than fewer positions on an axis. One input, three ways to treat it. A kernel with stride 2 jumps two positions each time, so it gives four outputs from eight, and its weights decide how two positions become one: learned. A max pool of two keeps the larger of each pair: a fixed rule, no weights. A global pool takes the whole axis into one value per channel: that is the aggregation of lesson 1, and the axis is gone from the shape. Padding and the output-shape formula are not in this course; your agent and the shape on the arrow in atlas tell you the length.

■ 01 — how far does one position see?

The receptive field: one position sees further with depth, pooling, wider kernels

The receptive field: how many input positions one output depends on. On the left, three kernels of three stacked: each layer adds one neighbour on each side, so the top cell sees seven positions. Put a pool of two between them and every layer above works on cells that are twice as wide, so the field grows much faster: eighteen positions with the same three kernels. On the right the third way: kernels of different sizes side by side on the same beat. A small one sees the edge of the spike, a middle one the whole QRS complex, a wide one the full beat. Concatenate their outputs on the channel axis and the next block sees all three scales at once.

■ 02 — how do you carry context along an axis?

goal 2.5

notebook 2.2

A state walks the axis, carrying context step by step

another geometry

A kernel assumes space: neighbours belong together, wherever they are.
A state assumes order: what came just before tells you what you need now, a Markov assumption.

what it costs

Step by step: h10 waits for h9, so no parallel windows.
Vanishing gradients: beyond roughly 100 to 200 steps the start hardly reaches the gradient any more.

A state is context carried along an ordered axis. At every step the same weights combine the new input with the state so far, and hand the result to the next step. It is another assumption about the data than a kernel makes, another geometry: a kernel assumes space, neighbours that belong together wherever they are; a state assumes order, that what came just before tells you what you need now. That is a Markov assumption. It costs two things. You compute step by step: step ten has to wait for step nine, where a kernel computes all its windows at once. And the gradient vanishes on the way back: what lies more than roughly 100 to 200 steps back hardly reaches the update, which is the red of x1 thinning out along h. The next part is how a gate controls that.

■ 03 — what is a skip?

goal 2.1

notebook 2.2

A skip is a route around a block, added back; the shape stays

invented for images, taken over by every architecture

2015ResNet · images
2016WaveNet · audio
2017Transformer · text
2019N-BEATS · series
lesson 1your head, skip=True

He, Zhang, Ren, Sun (2015). Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition. arXiv:1512.03385

What a skip is: a route around a block whose output is added to the block's output. Because it is added, the shape must stay the same, and it does. The block now only has to learn a correction to its input; when it has nothing to add, it can learn to output almost zero and the input passes unchanged. That is why a deep stack with skips does not lose what a shallow one had. He and colleagues introduced it in 2015 to train image networks of 152 layers, and since then nearly every architecture has one: WaveNet for audio, every block of a transformer, N-BEATS for series, and the skip switch you searched in lesson 1. Where it pays most is in deep stacks; what the class's own runs show about that is for the class dashboard, not for this slide.

The second instrument. A gate is a multiplier between 0 and 1 for every element, computed by the network from its input with a sigmoid. Here it is drawn as a gate that is not open everywhere: two rows fully open, two closed, and 75, 50 and 25 percent in between. Notation: the GRU on the notation card · goals 2.6 and 2.13

The kernel and the gate side by side, because they are easy to confuse. The kernel scans: a window slides along the axis, looks at neighbours and uses the same weights everywhere; the axis can get shorter. The gate weighs: one multiplier per element, between zero and one, and nothing moves, so the shape stays exactly the same. Put a gate on a state and it decides, per element, how much of the new candidate to take and how much of the old state to pass on: forget or remember. And the skip at the start of this part is a parallel route with the gate fixed at 1, always open. The notation card has the GRU written out, and goals 2.6 and 2.13 say what you should be able to explain.

A gate can filter noise

The gate multiplies element by element: where it is open the input passes, where it is closed the output is zero. In a GRU the same gate sits on the state.

A series with two events in a stream of noise. The gate is a row of multipliers, computed from the input: open at the two events, closed in between. Multiply the input by the gate, element by element, and what comes out is the events only; the faint grey line behind it is what would have passed without a gate. Data flows from top to bottom in this picture: input, gate, output. In a GRU the same gate sits on the state and decides how much of the new candidate to take and how much of the old state to pass on.

■ 03 — what does a gate decide?

goal 2.6

notebook 2.2

Squeeze-excite: a gate with a bottleneck

squeeze

One number per channel. Where is gone, how strong is left.

excite

Through a bottleneck it can only keep what usually goes together.

A face with cat ears is not a cat: the face turns the cat ears down. A hat over one ear: the other ear makes it expected.

Squeeze-excite is a gate on the channels of a convolutional layer, and each channel is a feature: a face, cat ears, fur, a left ear, a right ear. Squeeze pools away H and W, so every channel is one number: where is gone, how strong is left. Excite pushes those numbers through a narrow layer and back out, with a sigmoid at the end, and the result multiplies the channels. The narrowness is the point: it can only keep what usually goes together. How do you know that a person with cat ears is not a cat? The other features, a human face, turn the cat ears down. How do you know that I still have two ears under a hat? You fill in what is statistically plausible: one ear makes the other expected. That is what this block can do: it sees a left ear and no right ear, the squeeze loses where they were, and the excite brings both ears forward because that is what usually comes together.

■ 04 — what does a block assume about your data?

goal 2.7

notebook 2.1

Geometric deep learning: every block is a bet on geometry

kernel bets on locality

a pattern means the same wherever it occurs

pays: the shape of a beat, a texture

fails: what matters lies far apart

state bets on order

what came before changes what this means

pays: a rhythm across beats, a sentence

fails: long series: it forgets; or order means nothing, as in a set

trend + season bets on periodicity

the series repeats with a known period

pays: solar per day, sales per week

fails: no period, or the period drifts

Every block you have seen today assumes something about the data, and that assumption is a bet. A kernel bets on locality: a pattern means the same wherever it occurs, which pays on the shape of a heartbeat and fails when what matters lies far apart. A state bets on order: what came before changes the meaning, which pays on rhythm and fails on long series, where it forgets, or on data where order means nothing. A basis of trend and season bets on periodicity, which pays on solar power per day and fails when there is no period. This way of reasoning is geometric deep learning: grids, sequences, sets and graphs each come with their own geometry, and with their own bet (Bronstein et al. 2021, in the references).

■ 04 — offer the series as 1D or as 2D?

goal 2.8

notebook 2.1

Same data, new shape: the kernel's neighbours change

A heartbeat is 187 samples: (B, 187, 1), or folded as (B, 1, 11, 17).

Folded, a kernel also relates samples one row apart: about one period. About is enough. A period near the row width still lands in the kernel, every layer widens the receptive field, and two periods back is now two rows away instead of far down the series.

A design choice: state what you expect from the fold before you run it.

The same series offered in two shapes. As a row, a kernel of three sees the sample before and after. Fold the same 24 values into four rows of six and a three by three kernel also sees the samples six positions earlier and later. The data did not change; which positions count as neighbours did. For a heartbeat of 187 samples the fold is 11 by 17. What you add is periodicity: samples about one period apart become neighbours. The period does not have to match the row width. A pattern that is a little off still lands in the kernel, each extra layer widens the receptive field so a wider range of periods fits, and a pattern that falls just outside is still much closer than it was on the row: two periods back is two rows away. It pays if something that repeats matters. It is a design choice, so say what you expect before you run it.

■ 04 — how do you design with blocks?

goal 2.10

notebook 2.4

Type each step; atlas draws the shape on every arrow

your design script

# exercises/designs.py · one typed step per operation
beat  = Input("B S D", S=187, D=1)
local = Conv1D(beat, k=5,  out=16)    # (B,187,1) → (B,187,16)
wide  = Conv1D(beat, k=15, out=16)    # (B,187,1) → (B,187,16)
both  = Concat([local, wide], on="C") # → (B,187,32)
fewer = Pool1D(both, 2)               # → (B,93,32)
state = Recurrent(fewer, hidden=64)   # → (B,64)
kept  = Skip(Gate(64), state)         # → (B,64)
score = Linear(kept, out=2)           # → (B,2) the classifier

A design script says what you intend and what every step does to the shape. Read it in atlas before you train anything.

The last step is the classifier: (B, 2), one score for normal and one for abnormal per beat. With three classes it would be (B, 3).

the schema in atlas

How you design from today on. A design script has one typed step per operation, and each step says what it does to the shape. Atlas reads that script and draws the schema: the shape on every arrow, where the branches split, here into a narrow and a wide kernel, and where they join again, in the concat on the channel axis. The skip shows as a route around the gate, and the last Linear is the classifier: two outputs per beat, one per class; with three classes it would give three. Reading this picture is how you check a design before it costs you a training run, and how you explain it to someone else without your agent. Conv1D, Pool1D and Recurrent are names in a design script here; they arrive in vectormesh 0.14, so nothing on this slide claims they run today.

Ninety normal beats and ten abnormal: the majority classifier, your baseline, always says normal, scores 90% accuracy and finds no patient. Look at precision and recall on the rare class, and at F1 to put them in one number.

The metric fits the problem, and the problem is imbalance. In the heartbeat data most beats are normal. Take a hundred beats, ninety normal and ten abnormal: the majority classifier, which always says normal and is the baseline to beat, is right ninety times and has an accuracy of 90 percent, and it finds not one abnormal beat. The trained model on the left finds seven of ten, raises six false alarms, and has an accuracy of 91 percent, hardly better, while recall went from zero to 70 percent. Accuracy counts everything that is right, so the big class decides it. Precision asks: of the beats I flagged, how many were abnormal. Recall asks: of the abnormal beats, how many did I find. F1 is the harmonic mean of the two, so it is low when either is low. Which of them you care about depends on the cost: a missed patient costs a diagnosis, a false alarm a second look. The numbers here are illustrative; the real class balance is on the dataset card. And the rule for your design, as in every lesson: say beforehand what you expect and why, and be able to explain every choice without your agent present.

The close: the spine of the lesson, along the ranks of the tensor. A two-dimensional tensor, batch by sequence, is where a kernel on a single channel and the trend-and-season basis work. A three-dimensional tensor, batch by sequence by dimensionality, is where the kernel, the stride and pool, the global pool and the state work, and each of them does something visible to the shape: a kernel without padding shortens the sequence a little, a stride or pool halves every axis it works on, a global pool leaves one slice of D, a state leaves one hidden vector of H. Linear, gate and skip work on the last axis, whatever comes before it, so the red edge is the axis they use. A Linear usually goes from more features to fewer. A gate keeps the shape and turns some elements off, drawn in black. A skip keeps the shape and adds the correction the block learned, drawn in red. Next to each block is the bet it makes about your data. When you design today, every block in your design script has a place on this slide, and you should be able to say why its bet fits your data.
